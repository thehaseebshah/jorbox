# Jang

## How to Play

- **Age Limit:** 12+
- **Group Size:** 3+
- **Duration:** 60 minutes
- **Prep Time:** 60 minutes
- **Difficulty:** 5
- **Items Needed:** Map, tokens, paper, pencil

*Reference: How to Play Kriegsspiel*

### Introduction: Welcome to the Commander's Tent

This handbook is designed to give you a simple, accessible framework to run your first game of Jang.

The heart of Jang, and its most enjoyable aspect, isn't the minutia of combat. The real fun comes from "making the big decisions." It's about answering the big questions: "How am I going to get to the battle? Where am I going to commit my troops? Where am I going to put my reserve? Win, lose, or draw, what's my plan to get out?" That's where the magic is.

## 1. The Three Key Roles: Who's Who on the Battlefield

To play a game of Jang, you need a minimum of three people, each with a distinct role.

- **The Umpire:** Think of the Umpire as the game's referee, storyteller, and the engine that runs the world. They are the "artificial intelligence" for the troops on the map, handling the logical actions of subordinate commanders. Above all, the Umpire's number one mission is to "keep the game moving."
- **The Blue Player:** This player is one of the two opposing commanders, leading the forces of the "Blue" army.
- **The Red Player:** As the opposing commander, this player leads the "Red" army. This classic "Red vs. Blue" dynamic seen in countless games and simulations actually originates from Jang.

## 2. Your First Game's Toolkit: What You'll Need

For your first in-person game, you can remember the essential equipment with a simple "three sets of three" concept.

- **People:** You need a minimum of three people: one Umpire and two Players (Red and Blue). If you have more people, group them into parties — the Umpire party, the Blue Party, the Red Party, with one Ameer for each party.
- **Tables:** You'll need three tables — one for the Umpire and one for each player.
- **Maps:** You will need three identical maps. One map is for the Umpire, which shows the true position of all units. The other two maps are for the players. If you're playing digitally or remotely, you can use a shared digital map file or have the Umpire send photos or screenshots of what each player can see. You can also create a map on the ground with markings using what's commonly found in a park. You can also use household items.
- **Pieces:** You need three identical sets of playing pieces (blocks or tokens that represent your units). To keep your first game manageable, it's recommended to have a simple "order of battle" with "between 6 to 12 pieces" for each player.

### A "Double Blind" Setup

The reason for three tables, maps, and sets of pieces is to create a "double blind" experience. Each player's table only has their own pieces on their map. The Umpire's table is the master view, containing everything. This setup is crucial because it creates the "Fog of War" — the uncertainty and limited information of a real battlefield. Players can only see what their commander on the map can see, not the enemy's hidden dispositions.

## 3. Setting the Stage: Your Mission

A simple and clear scenario is the foundation of a great beginner's game. Follow these guidelines to set up your first battle.

- **Create a "McGuffin":** In filmmaking, a "McGuffin" is an object that drives the plot. For Jang, this means creating a simple objective for the players to fight over. This could be capturing a key town, securing a vital crossroads, or holding a strategic bridge. The most important thing is that the objectives for each side must conflict with one another to ensure a battle actually occurs.
- **Choose Historical Battles:** Like ones from our heroes like Khalid ibn al-Walid at Yarmuk, Saladin at Hattin, Tariq ibn Ziyad at Guadalete, Baybars on the Nile.
- **Winnable, Not Balanced:** Your scenario does not need to be perfectly fair or balanced. Asymmetrical forces and objectives can create fascinating challenges. However, the scenario "should be winnable for both sides."

## 4. The Rhythm of the Game: The Umpire's Cadence

The core gameplay loop is a four-step framework called the "Umpire's Cadence." This process repeats throughout the game, creating a steady rhythm of information, orders, and action.

### Step 1: The Report

Every turn begins with the Umpire providing each player with a report on what their commander sees and knows. This is how players receive information and pierce the Fog of War. There are three main ways to deliver a report.

| Report Type | Description | Key Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Screenshot/Drawn | A picture of the relevant part of the map. | "A picture is worth a thousand words" but the Umpire should crop it tightly to preserve the Fog of War. |
| Verbal | The Umpire describes the scene, e.g., "down the road you see red approaching in a column." | Can be very immersive and paint a picture, but can also take more time. |
| Written | A typed-out message, common in play-by-email games. | Can be prone to misinterpretation or typos if not carefully worded. |

### Step 2: The Orders

After receiving their report, each player gives orders to their forces. As a player, it's crucial to give clear and effective orders. As an Umpire, it's your job to guide players toward doing so.

- **Keep it Simple:** The best orders are simple and straightforward (e.g., "hold this position," "advance to that tree line").
- **Avoid Complexity:** Do not give highly detailed or conditional orders (e.g., "if they turn left, we shift right, but if they go straight, deploy skirmishers..."). As an umpire, you simply don't have the "mental RAM" or "the mental capacity to process all of that." If the orders are too complicated, they "are not going to be followed," and the player will get frustrated.
- **Trust the Umpire:** You don't need to give low-level commands like "open fire when in range." The Umpire, acting as the AI for your subordinate officers, will ensure your units take logical actions like defending themselves.

### Step 3: Process Orders (The Golden Rule)

This is where new umpires stumble most often. The key to a fair and realistic game is **simultaneous movement**.

The golden rule is a simple principle: **get all orders from all players first, and only then do you process them.**

It can be tempting to move Red's pieces as soon as Red gives an order, but this is a major pitfall. If you move one side's pieces before getting the other's orders, you create an awkward and unfair situation. Processing all orders simultaneously is what's "fair to the players and that's what's accurate."

### Step 4: Resolve Combat

- **Basic Roll:** The system uses a single six-sided die for most resolutions.
- **Artillery Example:** When artillery fires, a 1 is always a miss. A 6 is a direct hit. The Umpire interprets the results in between based on context. A 2 is "pretty much a miss," maybe wounding a single gunner. A 5 on a unit in the open is a "pretty good hit" that will have a real effect.
- **Umpire Discretion:** The Umpire modifies the outcome based on tactical factors. For example, if a unit in the woods is hit with a 6 (a direct hit), the umpire might reduce the effect to a 4 or 5 because the trees provide cover.
- **Morale:** After a unit takes hits, the Umpire might roll a "morale check" to see how the troops react. They might hold their ground, begin to waver, or fall back in good order.

Once combat is resolved and the pieces are moved, the cadence begins again with a new report based on the updated situation, creating a continuous loop that drives the game forward.

## 5. Conclusion: Your Next Adventure Awaits

You now have the complete framework to run your first game. Remember, the ultimate goal of Jang is to focus on "the Command Decisions" that define a battle. Avoid getting lost in the details and rules debates.

The best way to learn is to do it. Grab two friends, set up a simple scenario, and try your hand at umpiring. It's a rewarding experience that offers a unique look into the challenges of military command.
