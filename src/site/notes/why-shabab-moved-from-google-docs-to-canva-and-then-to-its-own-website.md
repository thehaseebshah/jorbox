# Why Shabab Moved From Google Docs to Canva and Then to Its Own Website

## Purpose

The Shabab Skills lessons have passed through three main stages: Google Drive and Google Docs, Canva, and finally Shabab's own website.
Each move solved real problems in the system that came before it.
Google Docs gave us a simple way to write and store lessons, Canva improved presentation and distribution, and our own website now combines those benefits with speed, organization, automation, ownership, and long-term independence.

## The Evolution of the Lesson System

### Stage One: Google Drive and Google Docs

The lessons were originally kept on Google Drive and written or shared through Google Docs.
This was a useful first step because the lessons were editable, stored online, and easy for the team to access without building a publishing system of its own.
Google Docs worked reasonably well as a writing and collaboration tool, but it was not designed to provide a polished learning experience for students.
Its long links, plain appearance, scattered documents, difficult video-update workflow, and frustrating formatting and image-placement tools eventually became significant limitations.

### Stage Two: Canva

Canva was the next step because it solved several immediate weaknesses of Google Docs.
It gave us shorter links that worked better in WhatsApp, more visually appealing lessons, and a much easier way to update videos without changing every previously shared link.
Reusable templates helped us keep lessons consistent, while Canva's visual canvas made formatting, positioning pictures, and combining multimedia much easier.
Its built-in design resources and broader export options also made lessons faster to produce and easier to distribute in different formats.
Canva was therefore an important bridge between basic cloud documents and a proper publishing platform.

However, Canva still left the lessons dependent on a proprietary service with an ongoing subscription cost for its paid capabilities.
Its pages were slow to load, the collection remained difficult to organize, and the lessons did not provide the animated and integrated experience we wanted.

### Stage Three: Obsidian, Digital Garden, and Shabab's Website

The current system keeps the source lessons locally as structured Markdown notes in Obsidian and publishes them through the Digital Garden and Shabab's own static website.
This gives Shabab a single system for writing, organizing, checking, maintaining, and publishing lessons.
It also gives us direct control over the files and the public learning experience instead of making either one dependent on Google Docs or Canva.

## Why We Moved From Google Docs to Canva

### 1. Shorter and More Practical Links for WhatsApp

Google Docs generated long URLs containing large document identifiers.
These links were awkward to place repeatedly in WhatsApp groups and private chats, visually cluttered messages, and were difficult for people to recognize or remember.
Because WhatsApp was one of our main ways of distributing lessons, link length was a practical issue rather than a merely cosmetic one.

Canva allowed us to share short and memorable addresses, such as `canva.link/lesson-one`.
A lesson name could be visible in the link itself, making the address easier to identify, copy, resend, and manage.
This made it much more convenient to drop lesson links into WhatsApp groups or individual chats.

### 2. More Beautiful and Appealing Lessons

Google Docs is primarily a writing and collaboration tool.
Its lesson pages looked plain and utilitarian, and we considered them too visually unattractive for the kind of student-facing experience we wanted to create.

Canva gave us much greater control over colors, layouts, typography, illustrations, and the overall visual presentation of a lesson.
The resulting lessons looked more polished, professional, and appealing.
This mattered because students are more likely to find a lesson approachable and engaging when it has been intentionally designed rather than presented as a plain working document.

### 3. Permanent Lesson Links With Replaceable Videos

The Google Docs and YouTube workflow made video corrections unnecessarily expensive in time and effort.
YouTube does not allow the underlying file of an uploaded video to be replaced while preserving that video's original URL.
If a video lesson needed to be corrected or improved, we first had to find and open the original video project on our own laptop or computer.
We then had to edit the project and render the complete video again.
Rendering could take a long time, particularly on a computer without a dedicated GPU.
After rendering, we had to delete or retire the old YouTube upload, upload the revised video as a new video, and wait for YouTube to process it.
The replacement received a new URL, so we then had to find every relevant Google Doc containing the previous link and change it manually.
Any overlooked document or old WhatsApp message could continue sending students to the outdated video.

Canva gave us a stable link that was separate from the exact version of the video behind it.
The video lived inside the Canva lesson, while the lesson's public link remained the same.
We could change or replace the video in place without changing the address that had already been added to lessons or shared in WhatsApp.
Canva also handled video processing in the cloud and rendered the result on the fly, so we did not need to perform a lengthy local render on a machine without a GPU.
This turned the public link into a permanent address while allowing the content behind that address to improve over time.

### 4. Reusable Lesson Templates and Consistent Design

Maintaining a consistent visual structure across many Google Docs was cumbersome.
Formatting choices could vary between documents, and recreating the same polished structure for every new lesson required repeated manual work.

Canva allowed us to build a reusable lesson template and duplicate it whenever we created new material.
The template could preserve the same colors, typography, spacing, page structure, and visual hierarchy across the curriculum.
This made production faster, gave the lessons a recognizable Shabab identity, and reduced inconsistencies between lessons created at different times.

### 5. Better Multimedia Composition

Google Docs was built around flowing text, so pictures, diagrams, icons, audio, and video often felt like additions to a document rather than integrated parts of its design.
It was difficult to compose several kinds of media together with precise visual relationships.

Canva provided a visual canvas on which text, photographs, illustrations, icons, diagrams, audio, and video could be arranged together intentionally.
We could decide exactly how the elements supported one another and construct a richer lesson around the media instead of forcing the media into a conventional document layout.

### 6. Easier Formatting and Image Placement

Google Docs was very hard to format when a lesson required anything beyond a straightforward text document.
Moving pictures around was particularly frustrating because changing an image's position or wrapping could shift nearby text, disturb spacing, alter page breaks, or disrupt the rest of the layout.
Achieving the intended result often required repeated adjustments and still produced an unpredictable page.

Canva made this work much easier through direct drag-and-drop positioning.
Pictures and other elements could be moved, resized, aligned, grouped, layered, and placed exactly where we wanted them without causing the rest of the lesson to reflow unexpectedly.
This gave us both faster editing and much more reliable control over the final appearance.

### 7. More Export and Distribution Options

Google Docs was primarily designed to remain a document, with options such as sharing the document itself or exporting it as a PDF.
That was limiting when the same lesson needed to be presented through different channels.

Canva could turn the same lesson into a presentation, PDF, set of page images, or video.
These formats made it easier to reuse material for printing, offline distribution, WhatsApp, presentations, or social media without rebuilding the lesson in a separate application.

### 8. Built-In Design Resources

Producing an attractive Google Doc often required finding visual material elsewhere, downloading it, importing it, and then struggling to position it inside the document.
Google Docs did not provide the same integrated range of design assets needed for fast visual production.

Canva included templates, stock photographs, illustrations, icons, shapes, fonts, and other drag-and-drop resources in one workspace.
This reduced the need to search across separate tools and made it possible to create polished lessons more quickly without specialist design software.

## Why Google Docs and Canva Were Still Not Enough

### Disconnected Files Created Organizational Chaos

Google Docs and Canva both treated lessons mainly as separate documents or designs.
Neither system gave the complete curriculum a strong, enforceable structure.
As the number of lessons grew, files became scattered and the overall collection became difficult to understand and maintain.

Duplicate lessons were created because there was no dependable conflict check when someone used a title that already existed.
Old versions were left to "rot" in forgotten locations even after a newer version had been created elsewhere.
It was often unclear which file was the authoritative version, which lessons belonged to which module, and whether a similarly named lesson was a duplicate or a genuinely different lesson.

This was not only a tidiness problem.
Poor organization increased the risk that students would receive outdated material and that team members would spend time recreating lessons that already existed.
It also made renumbering lessons, reorganizing modules, and checking the completeness of the curriculum unnecessarily difficult.

## Why We Moved From Canva to Our Own Website

### 1. A Much Faster Static Website

Canva lesson pages took a considerable amount of time to load.
The delay made opening a lesson feel heavy, especially for students using mobile devices, slower hardware, or weaker internet connections after following a link from WhatsApp.
A visually attractive lesson loses much of its value if the student has to wait too long for it to appear.

Shabab's website is a static website.
Its pages are generated in advance and can be delivered directly rather than assembled inside a heavy document or design viewer every time someone opens them.
This makes the website substantially faster and allows students to reach the lesson content with much less waiting.
The faster experience is more practical, more accessible, and better suited to repeated lesson use.

### 2. Clear Organization and Automatic Duplicate Prevention

The website turns the lessons from a loose collection of files into a structured curriculum.
Lessons can be placed in clearly defined modules, given consistent names and numbers, linked to related material, and maintained from one central source.
Students and maintainers no longer need to search through scattered Google Docs or Canva designs to determine where a lesson belongs.

Whenever a new lesson is created, the system checks for naming conflicts to determine whether another lesson with that name already exists.
This validation stops accidental duplication before it enters the curriculum.
It also encourages each lesson to have one canonical, current version instead of several competing copies in different locations.

Because the structure is represented in text and metadata, old lessons are easier to find, review, archive, update, or remove.
The system therefore addresses both obvious duplicates and the less visible problem of outdated lessons being forgotten in isolated folders or accounts.

### 3. A More Beautiful, Animated, and Exciting Experience

The Google Docs and Canva lesson formats we used were effectively static and had no meaningful animations in the delivered learning experience.
Even when the content was useful, the pages could feel like isolated documents rather than parts of a lively and coherent educational product.

Our own website can use beautiful, purposeful animations and interactive transitions throughout the interface.
Motion can make navigation, page changes, lesson elements, and user feedback feel polished and responsive while preserving a consistent visual identity.
These animations can be designed specifically for Shabab instead of being limited by the presentation rules of Google Docs or Canva.

The result is not only prettier than the old Google Docs pages but also more lively and exciting than either of the previous lesson formats.
When used carefully, animation gives students a stronger sense that they are exploring a modern learning platform rather than opening a static file.
Because we control the website, we can also optimize the animations so that visual excitement does not require the slow page loading we experienced with Canva.

### 4. One Integrated Learning Experience

Google Docs and Canva provided individual lesson pages, but our website can present the entire curriculum as one connected experience.
We control the navigation, search, module structure, internal links, mobile layout, typography, branding, accessibility, and behavior of every lesson.
We can also introduce interactive features in the future without waiting for a third-party platform to support them.

A stable website address can continue pointing to the current lesson while the lesson's text, videos, organization, and design evolve behind it.
This preserves the stable-link advantage we gained from Canva while bringing the content into a faster and better-organized system.

### 5. Greater Student Privacy

When students open lessons through Google Docs or Canva, those companies deliver the pages and determine what platform cookies, telemetry, and other visitor data are collected.
Shabab has limited visibility into or control over those data-collection decisions.
Students may therefore interact with a large third-party platform even when they only intend to read a Shabab lesson.

On our own website, Shabab can decide which analytics, cookies, embedded services, and third-party scripts are genuinely necessary.
We can minimize tracking, select privacy-respecting tools, and explain our practices directly instead of automatically accepting the policies of a document or design platform.
An independently operated website does not make privacy automatic, but it puts the relevant decisions under Shabab's control.

### 6. Site-Wide Design Changes From One Place

Canva templates helped us start new lessons with a consistent design, but each duplicated Canva lesson became a separate design.
If we later changed a font, color, spacing rule, header, or other shared element, old designs still had to be opened and updated individually.
The same fragmentation existed across separate Google Docs.

Our website uses shared layouts, components, and styles across the complete lesson library.
A change to a central template or stylesheet can update every lesson simultaneously without editing each lesson's content.
This makes it practical to refresh Shabab's visual identity, improve accessibility, or correct a common design problem across the entire website in one controlled change.

### 7. Better Support for Multiple Languages

Maintaining translations in Google Docs or Canva generally requires separate documents or designs for each language.
Every correction, visual adjustment, or structural change must then be repeated across those copies, making it easy for translated versions to fall out of sync.

Our website can organize multiple language versions around the same lesson structure.
It can support languages such as English and Urdu, provide language switching, and apply right-to-left layouts where required while preserving consistent navigation and design.
This gives Shabab a more scalable foundation for serving different language communities in the future.

### 8. No Dependence on Canva's Subscription

Canva has a recurring subscription cost for access to its paid features, premium assets, brand tools, expanded storage, and other capabilities used in a serious production workflow.
Continuing to maintain the lessons inside Canva therefore ties part of Shabab's publishing process to an active paid plan.
If the subscription price rises or the plan ends, important tools or assets may become restricted.

Our website still has legitimate costs such as its domain, hosting, and maintenance, but publishing the curriculum no longer requires an ongoing Canva subscription.
Shabab pays for infrastructure it controls instead of paying continuously for permission to edit and present its own lesson collection inside Canva.

## Why the Local-First System Is the Right Long-Term Foundation

### 1. Ownership, Protection From Account Loss, and Censorship Resilience

Google and Canva ultimately control accounts hosted on their platforms.
They can suspend or close an account, remove content, change their moderation policies, restrict features, or make an automated decision that blocks access.
This is a real concern for Shabab because we have experienced account closure before.
If the only copies of the lessons live inside a closed account, we can lose access to all of that data and may have no dependable way to recover it.

In the current setup, the original lesson files belong to Shabab and remain on storage that we control.
They can be backed up to multiple drives, repositories, or other locations, so access to the curriculum does not depend on the continued permission of one company.
A platform can stop serving one copy, but it cannot erase every independently held source file.

This ownership is also important for the future of Shabab if censorship becomes a concern.
A proprietary platform may restrict educational material because of new policies, automated moderation, political pressure, regional rules, or a changed interpretation of acceptable content.
With a local archive and an independently controlled website, Shabab can move to another host, create mirrors, or republish the material elsewhere.
The setup does not make Shabab completely censorship-proof because hosting providers, domain registrars, and network infrastructure can still impose restrictions.
It does, however, provide censorship resilience by ensuring that no single content platform can permanently erase Shabab's educational archive.

### 2. Lessons Can Be Manipulated Easily With AI and Automation

The lessons are stored as structured, text-based Markdown files rather than being trapped inside visual designs or scattered cloud documents.
Plain text is easy for AI tools and automation scripts to read, analyze, transform, and validate.
This makes changes across the whole curriculum possible without opening and editing every lesson manually.

For example, we can change many lesson titles in one operation, renumber an entire sequence, move lessons between modules, reorganize the modules themselves, add or correct metadata, standardize formatting, and update repeated wording across hundreds of files.
Automation can also scan lesson names before creation, report conflicts, identify likely duplicates, locate broken links, and find inconsistent numbering.
AI can help classify lessons, suggest relationships, improve language, or prepare large migrations while scripts apply deterministic rules safely and consistently.

These capabilities save time now and become increasingly valuable as the number of lessons grows.
A change that might require days of repetitive editing in Google Docs or Canva can become a reviewed script or AI-assisted batch operation that runs in seconds.

### 3. Offline Access and Reliability

The local Obsidian vault remains usable without an internet connection.
The team can read, write, edit, link, and organize lessons even when Google Docs, Canva, the hosting provider, or the local internet connection is unavailable.
Work does not have to stop because a third-party service is down or a network connection is unreliable.

Offline access also improves responsiveness because opening and editing a local text file does not require a remote design application to load first.
When a connection returns, the prepared changes can be synchronized or published to the website.

### 4. Freedom From Vendor Lock-In

The strategic advantage of the current system is that the source material is stored in ordinary Markdown files.
Even though individual applications in the workflow may be proprietary or replaceable, the lessons themselves are not locked into a proprietary document or design format.

Markdown can be opened by many editors, processed by many programming languages, placed under version control, converted into other formats, or published by a different website generator.
If Shabab stops using Obsidian, the files remain readable.
If Shabab changes the Digital Garden system, website framework, hosting provider, or domain, the content can move without being recreated from scratch.

This gives Shabab the freedom to select future tools according to its needs rather than remaining with a platform because leaving it would mean losing or manually rebuilding the curriculum.

### 5. One Source of Truth

The Obsidian notes are the source material from which the public website is published.
The internal knowledge base and the student-facing lessons therefore do not need to be maintained as separate, manually synchronized copies.

A correction can be made once in the source note and then published to the website.
This reduces the chance that one Google Doc, one Canva design, and one public link will each contain a different version of the same lesson.
It also makes it clear which copy is authoritative: the structured source file in the curriculum.

A single source of truth complements stable public links.
The URL can remain consistent for students while the canonical lesson behind it receives corrections, improved videos, better organization, or visual enhancements.

### 6. Complete Control Over the Learning Experience

An external document platform decides how documents are displayed and which features are available.
Our own website lets Shabab decide how students discover, open, read, and move between lessons.

We control navigation, search, module pages, related-lesson links, typography, colors, branding, responsive mobile behavior, accessibility, loading behavior, and animations.
We can design a consistent experience across the entire curriculum instead of styling each lesson as an isolated document.
We can also add future interactive features, progress tools, richer media, or other learning aids without waiting for Google or Canva to add the required capability.

This control allows the public experience to remain both beautiful and practical.
We can combine Canva's emphasis on visual appeal with the speed, consistency, animations, and integration of a purpose-built product.

### 7. Better Scalability and Long-Term Maintenance

Text-based lessons are easier to search, compare, update, validate, back up, and restore than a large collection of disconnected documents or designs.
They can be tracked with version control, which records what changed and makes it possible to review or reverse an incorrect edit.
A deleted or damaged lesson can be recovered from backups or history instead of depending entirely on a platform's account-recovery process.

The structured system makes bulk maintenance practical as Shabab grows.
Names and numbering can follow consistent rules, modules can be reorganized without losing the underlying content, and automated checks can catch conflicts before they spread.
Old versions can be identified deliberately rather than being left to rot unnoticed, and every lesson can have a clear place in the curriculum.

The result is a system that becomes more useful as the library grows instead of becoming progressively more chaotic.
It reduces repetitive work, prevents avoidable errors, and gives future maintainers a clear foundation on which to build.

## What Each Move Accomplished

### Google Drive and Google Docs

Google Drive and Google Docs gave Shabab an accessible starting point for storing, writing, and sharing digital lessons.
Their main weaknesses were long links, unattractive student-facing pages, scattered organization, difficult video replacement, frustrating formatting and picture placement, duplicate files, limited multimedia composition, and dependence on a Google account.

### Canva

Canva improved the visual quality of lessons, provided shorter WhatsApp-friendly links, and made it possible to update cloud-processed videos behind a stable public address.
It also gave us reusable templates, easier formatting and image placement, richer multimedia composition, built-in design resources, and multiple export formats for different distribution channels.
Its main weaknesses were slow loading, an ongoing subscription cost, limited control over student privacy, continued dependence on a proprietary account, weak curriculum-wide organization, outdated and duplicate files, and a static lesson experience without the website-level animations we wanted.

### Obsidian, Digital Garden, and Shabab's Website

The current system combines local ownership with public web publishing.
It provides fast static pages, organized modules, duplicate detection, stable lesson addresses, attractive animations, AI and automation support, offline editing, portable Markdown files, one source of truth, centrally managed site-wide design, multilingual possibilities, greater control over student privacy, and direct control over the entire learning experience.

## Conclusion

The move from Google Docs to Canva and then to Shabab's own website was not simply a sequence of design preferences.
Each transition responded to concrete operational problems discovered while creating and distributing real lessons.

Google Docs was useful for basic writing and cloud access, but its links, appearance, organization, formatting, image placement, multimedia limitations, and video workflow did not scale.
Canva made lessons easier to share, more attractive, easier to format, consistent through reusable templates, richer in multimedia, simpler to export, and much easier to update when videos changed.
Its built-in design resources also accelerated production, but Canva remained slow, subscription-based, proprietary, poorly organized at curriculum scale, limited our control over student privacy, and was visually static in delivery.
The local-first Obsidian, Digital Garden, and static website system keeps the best lessons from those stages while solving their deeper limitations through shared site-wide design, multilingual capabilities, and greater privacy control.

Most importantly, the new system gives Shabab control over both its educational archive and its future.
The lessons can remain available offline, survive account closures, be moved if censorship or platform restrictions arise, be improved in bulk through AI and automation, and be published through a fast, organized, beautiful, and evolving website.
