### Why Shabab Moved From Google Docs To Canva And Then To Its Own Website

### Purpose

The lessons of Shabab Skills have evolved across three major phases: Google Drive & Google Docs, Canva, and ultimately Shabab's own custom website.
Each transition solved real challenges presented by the previous system.
Google Docs provided a simple way to write and store lessons, Canva enhanced visual presentation and distribution, and our custom website now combines these benefits with high speed, structured organization, automation, ownership, and long-term independence.

### Evolution of the Lesson System

##### Phase 1: Google Drive & Google Docs
Initially, lessons were stored on Google Drive and written or shared via Google Docs.
This was a useful first step because lessons were editable, stayed saved online, and the team could access them easily without building a custom publishing platform.
Google Docs was suitable for writing and collaboration, but it was not designed to deliver a polished learning experience for Murabbis (mentors).
Its long links, basic visual appearance, scattered documents, cumbersome video-update workflow, and frustrating formatting and image repositioning tools became major bottlenecks.

##### Phase 2: Canva
Canva was the next step because it eliminated several immediate weaknesses of Google Docs.
It provided short links for WhatsApp, more beautifully designed lessons, and an easy way to update videos without changing previously shared links.
Reusable templates maintained consistency across lessons, while Canva's visual canvas made formatting, image placement, and multimedia integration easy.
Its built-in design resources and multiple export options also accelerated lesson preparation and distribution across different formats.
Thus, Canva served as an important bridge between basic cloud documents and a full-fledged publishing platform.

However, Canva kept the lessons dependent on a proprietary service and required an ongoing paid subscription for its premium features.
Its pages loaded slowly, organizing the full collection remained difficult, and lessons could not offer the dynamic and interactive experience we desired.

##### Phase 3: Obsidian, Digital Garden, and Shabab's Custom Website
The current system stores original lessons locally as structured Markdown notes in Obsidian and publishes them via Digital Garden and Shabab's own static website.
This gives Shabab a unified system to write, organize, validate, maintain, and publish lessons.
It also ensures direct ownership over files and the public learning experience, eliminating dependence on Google Docs or Canva.

#### Why We Moved From Google Docs To Canva

##### 1. Short and Practical Links for WhatsApp
Google Docs generated long URLs containing complex document identifiers.
Pasting these links repeatedly into WhatsApp groups and private chats was cumbersome, made messages look cluttered, and was hard for users to recognize or remember.
Since WhatsApp was a primary channel for distributing lessons, link length was a practical usability issue, not just an aesthetic one.

Canva allowed us to share short, memorable addresses like `canva.link/lesson-one`.
The lesson title could be visible in the link, making it much easier to identify, copy, forward, and manage in WhatsApp groups and individual chats.

##### 2. More Beautiful and Engaging Lessons
Google Docs is primarily a tool for word processing and collaboration.
Its lesson pages looked plain and purely utilitarian, whereas we wanted to create a more engaging experience for Murabbis.

Canva gave us far greater control over colors, layouts, typography, illustrations, and the overall visual presentation of lessons.
As a result, lessons looked far more polished, professional, and visually appealing.
This was crucial because a thoughtfully designed lesson feels more accessible and engaging to Murabbis compared to a plain working document.

##### 3. Permanent Lesson Links With Replaceable Videos
The workflow using Google Docs and YouTube made updating videos unnecessarily expensive in terms of time and effort.
YouTube does not allow replacing the original video file while keeping the same URL.
If a video lesson needed correction or improvement, one had to find and open the original video project on a local laptop or computer, edit the project, and re-render the entire video.
Without a dedicated GPU, rendering could take a significant amount of time.
After rendering, the old YouTube video had to be deleted or unlisted, the new video uploaded, and YouTube's processing time waited out.
Because the new video received a new URL, every corresponding Google Doc had to be manually located and updated with the new link.
Any overlooked document or old WhatsApp message would continue directing Murabbis to the outdated video.

Canva provided a stable link decoupled from the specific version of the underlying video.
The video resided inside the Canva lesson while the lesson's public link remained unchanged.
We could replace the video in place without changing the link shared in lessons or WhatsApp.
Furthermore, Canva processed and rendered video content in the cloud on the fly, eliminating the need for long local rendering sessions without a GPU.
Thus, the public link became a permanent address while the underlying content could be updated over time.

##### 4. Reusable Templates and Consistent Design
Maintaining a consistent visual structure across many Google Docs was difficult.
Formatting varied across different documents, and recreating the same polished layout for every new lesson required continuous manual effort.

In Canva, we could build reusable lesson templates and clone them for every new piece of content.
These templates maintained uniform colors, typography, spacing, page structure, and visual hierarchy throughout the curriculum.
This sped up creation, gave lessons a distinct Shabab identity, and minimized stylistic discrepancies across different authors and times.

##### 5. Superior Multimedia Composition
Google Docs was designed around continuous body text, making images, diagrams, icons, audio, and video feel like attachments rather than integrated design elements.
Combining different types of media with clear visual relationships was difficult.

Canva offered a visual canvas where text, images, illustrations, icons, diagrams, audio, and video could be thoughtfully composed together.
We could decide exactly how different elements reinforced one another, creating a richer lesson experience around media rather than squeezing media into a traditional text document.

##### 6. Easier Formatting and Precise Image Placement
Formatting in Google Docs became frustrating whenever a lesson required design beyond plain text.
Moving images was particularly troublesome because changing an image's position or text wrapping could shift adjacent text, spacing, page breaks, and break the entire layout.
Achieving the desired result required repetitive adjustments, and the final output remained unpredictable.

Canva made this effortless through direct drag-and-drop.
Images and other elements could be moved, resized, aligned, grouped, layered, and placed precisely where intended without disturbing the rest of the lesson layout.
This accelerated editing and provided reliable control over the final visual output.

##### 7. Expanded Export and Distribution Options
Google Docs was intended to remain primarily a document, shareable directly or exportable as a PDF.
When the same lesson needed to be presented across different channels, this narrow format scope was limiting.

Canva could turn a single lesson into a presentation, a PDF, a set of page images, or a video.
This made it easy to reuse content for printing, offline distribution, WhatsApp, live presentations, or social media without recreating it in another app.

##### 8. Integrated Design Resources
Creating a visually appealing Google Doc required searching for visual assets elsewhere, downloading them, importing them, and then attempting to align them within the document.
Google Docs provided no built-in design assets for rapid visual production.

Canva provided templates, stock photos, illustrations, icons, shapes, fonts, and drag-and-drop assets all in one place.
This reduced context switching across tools and allowed us to quickly create polished lessons without specialized design software.

##### Organizational Chaos From Unlinked Files
Both Google Docs and Canva treated lessons mostly as standalone documents or individual designs.
Neither system enforced a strong, unified structure across the entire curriculum.
As the number of lessons grew, files became scattered, making the overall collection difficult to understand and manage.

Without reliable conflict checks to flag existing titles, duplicate lessons were created.
When a new version was created elsewhere, older versions continued to "rot" in forgotten locations.
It was often unclear which file was the authoritative source, which module a lesson belonged to, or whether a similarly named file was a duplicate or a distinct lesson.

This was not merely an organizational neatness issue.
Poor organization increased the risk of Murabbis receiving outdated material and team members re-implementing existing lessons from scratch.
Renumbering lessons, reorganizing modules, and auditing curriculum completeness were also unnecessarily difficult.

#### Why We Moved From Canva To Our Custom Website

##### 1. A Significantly Faster Static Website
Canva lesson pages took noticeable time to load.
Opening a lesson via a WhatsApp link felt heavy, especially for Murabbis on mobile devices, low-spec hardware, or weaker internet connections.
If a Murabbi has to wait too long to view a beautifully designed lesson, much of its value is lost.

Shabab's website is a static website.
Its pages are pre-built and served directly to users without assembling heavy documents or rendering design viewers on every request.
This makes the website remarkably fast, allowing Murabbis to access lessons with minimal waiting.
A fast experience is far more practical, accessible, and suitable for frequent reference.

##### 2. Clear Organization and Automated Duplicate Prevention
The website transforms a loose collection of lessons into a structured curriculum.
Lessons are organized into clear modules, given standardized titles and numbers, hyperlinked to related content, and managed from a single central source.
Murabbis and administrators no longer need to search through scattered Google Docs or Canva designs to locate a lesson.

Whenever a new lesson is created, the system checks for naming conflicts against existing titles.
This validation prevents accidental duplicates from entering the curriculum.
Instead of multiple competing versions scattered across locations, every lesson maintains a single canonical, current version.

Because structure is defined in text and metadata, searching, auditing, backing up, updating, or removing old lessons is effortless.
Thus, the system solves both duplicate lesson creation and forgotten legacy files.

##### 3. Activity Reuse Via Embedded Notes
Every activity is now saved as an independent Markdown note that can be embedded into any lesson rather than copy-pasting full content.
The canonical note of a single activity can be included across different lesson batches of Shabab, as well as lessons for other Majalis (gatherings).
This eliminates the need to rewrite, copy, or maintain separate versions of the activity for each batch or Majlis.

When instructions, required materials, or learning objectives of an activity are updated, edits are made solely in its source file. Upon the next publish, all lessons embedding that activity automatically display the updated content.
This creates a scalable shared activity library, reduces redundant effort and inconsistencies, and allows different lesson batches or Majalis to assemble these activities into customized sequences.

Additionally, through backlink tracking, each activity note clearly reveals which specific Shabab lessons and lesson batches it is attached to. This bi-directional visibility makes auditing curriculum coverage and conducting future analysis far easier.

##### 4. A More Beautiful, Dynamic, and Engaging Experience
The Google Docs and Canva lesson formats we used were essentially static and lacked meaningful animations within the learning experience.
Even useful content could feel like an isolated document rather than a living, cohesive educational product.

Our custom website can utilize tasteful, functional animations and interactive transitions across the entire interface.
Motion enhances navigation, page transitions, lesson components, and user feedback while maintaining a unified visual identity.
These animations are custom-designed specifically for Shabab rather than constrained by third-party platforms.

The result is not only more visually pleasing than old Google Docs, but far more dynamic and engaging than both previous formats.
When used thoughtfully, UI motion gives Murabbis the feel of navigating a modern learning platform rather than reading a static file.
Because we control the website, animations can be optimized so visual elegance does not come at the cost of slow loading times like Canva.

##### 5. An Integrated Learning Experience
Google Docs and Canva delivered isolated lesson pages, whereas our website presents the entire curriculum as a unified experience.
Navigation, search, module structure, internal linking, mobile responsive layouts, typography, branding, accessibility, and UI behavior are fully under our control.
We can also introduce interactive features in the future without waiting for third-party platform updates.

The website's permanent URLs continue pointing to current lessons while text, videos, structure, and design evolve behind them.
Thus, Canva's benefit of stable links is preserved within a faster, more organized system.

##### 6. Greater Privacy for Murabbis
When Murabbis open lessons on Google Docs or Canva, those platforms serve the pages and determine which cookies, telemetry, and user data are collected.
Shabab's visibility and control over these privacy practices were minimal.
Thus, even a Murabbi simply reading a Shabab lesson interacted with a large third-party data collection apparatus.

On our own website, Shabab decides which analytics, cookies, embedded services, and third-party scripts are necessary.
We can minimize tracking, select privacy-conscious tools, and establish clear policies rather than inheriting platform defaults.
An independent website does not automatically guarantee privacy, but it places all relevant privacy choices under Shabab's control.

##### 7. Sitewide Design Updates From a Single Location
Canva templates gave new lessons a consistent starting point, but every cloned Canva design became an isolated file.
Updating fonts, colors, spacing, headers, or shared components later required opening and editing each old design individually.
Dispersed Google Docs suffered from the exact same fragmentation.

Our website uses shared layouts, components, and stylesheets across the entire lesson collection.
A single change in a central template or stylesheet automatically applies across all lessons without touching individual content files.
This makes updating Shabab's visual identity, improving accessibility, or fixing layout bugs sitewide a single, streamlined change.

##### 8. Superior Support for Multiple Languages
Maintaining translations in Google Docs or Canva typically required creating separate documents or designs for each language.
Every revision, visual tweak, or structural update then had to be duplicated across all copies, increasing the risk of language versions drifting apart.

Our website can organize multi-language versions around a single underlying lesson structure.
It natively supports English and Urdu, language switching, and Right-to-Left (RTL) layouts with consistent navigation and styling.
This provides a scalable foundation to serve diverse linguistic communities in the future.

##### 9. Elimination of Ongoing Canva Subscription Costs
Canva's advanced features, premium assets, brand tools, additional storage, and export capabilities require an ongoing paid subscription.
Keeping lessons in Canva tied a core part of Shabab's publishing workflow to an active subscription plan.
Price increases or subscription lapses could restrict access to vital tools or assets.

While hosting, domain registration, and maintenance incur real costs, running our custom website does not require an ongoing Canva subscription.
Shabab invests in infrastructure it owns, rather than paying continuously for permission to edit and present its own lessons inside Canva.

##### 10. Simple Markdown Formatting Without Setting Fonts, Sizes, or Colors
Writing in Google Docs and Canva required making continuous visual decisions—choosing fonts, font sizes, colors, margins, alignments, and layout details.
These styling choices distracted from content creation and caused formatting inconsistencies across different authors and documents.

Markdown keeps writing simple and content-focused.
Authors structure headings, lists, links, emphasis, and quotes using plain text symbols without styling individual elements manually.
When published, the website's unified design system automatically enforces correct typography, font sizes, colors, spacing, and responsive behavior.
As a result, lessons are written faster, remain visually consistent, and can be maintained effortlessly even by non-designers.

##### 11. An Offline-First Publishing System for Lessons and Future Blogs
The current system is not merely a website for viewing lessons; it is an offline-first workflow for creating and publishing content.
We can write, edit, structure, and organize lessons and future blog posts locally in Obsidian as Markdown notes, whether an internet connection is available or not.
The internet is only needed when syncing completed changes to publish them on the public website, not while brainstorming, drafting, editing, or organizing.

Google Docs offers an offline mode, but its primary source remains a cloud document; Canva is similarly tied to its online service.
In our system, the authoritative original file resides locally on our machines from day one.
Poor or disconnected internet does not interrupt publishing work, providing a resilient foundation for today's lessons and tomorrow's blog posts.

#### Why a Local-First System Is the Right Long-Term Foundation

##### 1. Ownership, Protection From Account Loss, and Censorship Resilience
Google and Canva exercise ultimate control over accounts hosted on their platforms.
They can suspend or terminate accounts, delete content, modify content policies, restrict features, or revoke access through automated decisions.
This is a real concern for Shabab because we have experienced an account termination event in the past.
If lessons exist solely inside a locked account, access to all data can be lost without a reliable recovery path.

In our current system, the original lesson files are owned by Shabab and stored on storage we control.
They can be backed up across multiple drives, repositories, and locations, so access to the curriculum does not depend on any single company's permission.
A platform can stop hosting a copy, but it cannot erase our locally saved source files.

This ownership becomes even more critical if censorship becomes an issue in the future.
A proprietary platform could restrict educational content due to policy changes, automated flags, political pressure, regional regulations, or changing terms of service.
With local storage and an independent website, Shabab can migrate hosting, create mirrors, or republish content elsewhere.
While local-first storage does not make Shabab entirely immune to network-level blockages (such as hosting or registrar actions), it guarantees that no single platform can permanently delete Shabab's educational repository.

##### 2. Effortless Lesson Refactoring via AI and Automation
Instead of being trapped inside visual designs or proprietary cloud documents, lessons are stored as structured, plain-text Markdown files.
Plain text can be easily parsed, analyzed, transformed, and validated by AI tools and automation scripts.
This allows us to perform curriculum-wide refactoring without opening files one by one.

For example, in a single automated operation we can update lesson titles, renumber entire series, move lessons between modules, reorganize modules, fix metadata, standardize formatting, or replace repeated terminology across hundreds of files.
Automation can validate lesson titles before creation, detect duplicate naming, identify broken links, and catch inconsistent numbering.
AI can assist in categorizing lessons, suggesting cross-references, refining language, and preparing major migrations, while scripts enforce deterministic rules cleanly and consistently.

These capabilities save time today and grow increasingly valuable as the curriculum expands.
Changes that would take days of manual editing in Google Docs or Canva can be executed in seconds using tested scripts or AI assistance.

##### 3. Offline Access and Reliable Productivity
The local Obsidian vault remains fully functional without an internet connection.
Even if Google Docs, Canva, web hosting, or local internet connections are down, the team can read, write, edit, link, and organize lessons uninterrupted.
Work never stalls due to third-party service outages or weak network connectivity.

Because local text files open instantly without loading heavy web applications, offline productivity is also significantly faster.
Once internet connectivity is restored, completed changes can be synced and published to the website seamlessly.

##### 4. Freedom From Vendor Lock-In
A core advantage of the current system is that content is stored in open Markdown files.
While individual tools in the workflow may change, the lessons themselves are not trapped in proprietary document or design formats.

Markdown files can be read by countless text editors, processed by any programming language, tracked in version control, converted to other formats, or published using different static site generators.
Even if Shabab stops using Obsidian, the files remain fully accessible and usable.
If we change Digital Garden, web frameworks, hosting providers, or domains, content can be migrated without re-authoring.

Shabab can choose future tools based on needs, without staying on a platform out of fear that leaving will destroy or force a manual rebuild of the curriculum.

##### 5. A Single Source of Truth
The local Obsidian notes serve as the single canonical source from which the public website is published.
There is no need to maintain separate, manually synchronized copies for internal knowledge and public viewing.

An edit is made once in the source note and published to the website.
This eliminates discrepancies where a Google Doc, a Canva design, and a public link represent different versions of the same lesson.
It remains unambiguous that the structured note in the vault is the authoritative version.

A single source of truth complements permanent public URLs: the URL remains stable for Murabbis, while the underlying canonical lesson receives corrections, updated videos, improved structure, and visual enhancements seamlessly.

##### 6. Full Control Over the Educational Experience
Third-party document platforms dictate how content is displayed and what features are supported.
On our custom website, Shabab controls how Murabbis search, open, read, and navigate through lessons.

Navigation, search functionality, module pages, cross-lesson linking, typography, color schemes, branding, mobile responsiveness, accessibility, loading speed, and UI animations are entirely within our control.
We can design a unified experience for the whole curriculum rather than treating each lesson as a standalone document.
Future interactive features, progress tracking, rich media, and learning tools can be added directly without waiting for platform features from Google or Canva.

This control keeps the public experience both beautiful and functional.
We combine Canva's visual appeal with the speed, consistency, dynamic motion, and cohesion of a purpose-built educational website.

##### 7. Superior Scalability and Long-Term Maintainability
Text-based lessons are far easier to search, compare, update, audit, backup, and restore than large collections of unlinked documents or visual designs.
Files can be managed in version control (Git), recording every revision and enabling easy code reviews or rollbacks if an error occurs.
A deleted or corrupted lesson can be restored instantly from Git history or backups, without relying on platform account recovery processes.

A structured system makes bulk maintenance practical as Shabab grows.
Naming and numbering follow strict rules, modules can be restructured without losing source content, and automated checks catch errors before deployment.
Legacy notes are systematically managed rather than rotting unnoticed, ensuring every lesson has a clear place within the curriculum.

As the lesson repository grows, the system becomes more valuable rather than more chaotic.
It minimizes redundant effort, prevents avoidable mistakes, and provides a clear, maintainable foundation for future administrators.

#### What Each Transition Achieved

##### Google Drive & Google Docs
Google Drive and Google Docs provided Shabab with a simple initial way to write, store, and share digital lessons.
Their major limitations were long links, plain and unengaging visual presentation, scattered organization, difficult video updating, frustrating formatting and image positioning, duplicate files, limited multimedia composition, and complete reliance on Google accounts.

##### Canva
Canva improved the visual quality of lessons, introduced short links for WhatsApp, and enabled updating cloud-rendered videos behind stable public addresses.
It also provided reusable templates, easier formatting and image placement, rich multimedia composition, built-in design assets, and multiple export formats.
Its major limitations were slow loading times, ongoing subscription costs, limited control over user privacy, reliance on a proprietary service, weak curriculum-level organization, legacy file clutter, and a static presentation lacking modern web animations.

##### Obsidian, Digital Garden, and Shabab's Custom Website
The current system combines local ownership with public web publishing.
It delivers fast static pages, structured modules, automated duplicate prevention, permanent lesson URLs, activity reuse across batches and Majalis via embedded notes, engaging UI animations, AI and automation refactoring, simple Markdown formatting without manual styling, offline creation and editing for lessons and future blogs, portable Markdown files, a single source of truth, centrally managed sitewide design, multi-language capabilities, greater privacy control for Murabbis, and direct ownership over the entire learning experience.

#### Conclusion

The evolution from Google Docs to Canva and ultimately to Shabab's custom website was not a matter of superficial design preferences.
Each transition solved real operational and pedagogical challenges encountered during lesson creation and distribution.

Google Docs served well for basic writing and cloud access, but its links, layout, organization, formatting, image tools, multimedia constraints, and video workflows were unsuited for scale.
Canva made sharing, styling, formatting, templating, multimedia integration, exporting, and video updating significantly easier. Its integrated design tools accelerated production, but Canva remained slow, subscription-dependent, proprietary, unorganized at the curriculum level, privacy-restricted, and static in presentation.
The local-first Obsidian, Digital Garden, and static website architecture resolves these core weaknesses while retaining the best advantages of previous phases through unified design, multi-language support, and enhanced privacy.

Most importantly, the new system gives Shabab complete ownership over its educational repository and its future.
Lessons remain accessible offline, are protected against account loss, can be migrated if faced with censorship or platform restrictions, can be refactored at scale using AI and automation, and are published on a fast, structured, beautiful, and continuously evolving website.
